If you have searched for "Dragon Grove" and landed on a page about a Roblox map, a Steam survival title, or a match-three mobile game, you have found the wrong game. Dragon Grove is a mixed-reality spatial puzzle game from Schell Games, built as a client project with Meta. It is played on Meta Quest 3 headsets and up, entirely with native hand tracking, no controller required. This article sticks to what the developer has actually documented about the game. You can confirm the essentials on the official [Dragon Grove page at Schell Games](https://schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove).

## What Dragon Grove Is, According to the Developer

Schell Games lists Dragon Grove under Casual, Puzzle, and Entertainment, built in Unity for Mixed Reality, aimed at both family and adult players. It is free to play, expected in spring 2027, and designed for seated, cozy, low-stress sessions. The core loop is matching: you match leaves and flowers with your hands, awaken magical dragon plants, and grow your grove. Along the way you unlock dragon abilities, bond with the dragons you meet, and expand what your grove can hold.

Two details matter for anyone comparing this to other games with similar names. First, it is not a Roblox experience. Second, it is not the Steam game Dragon's Grove from Frozen Flame, and it is not Merge Dragons. Confusing these is easy because the names overlap, but the mechanics, platforms, and developers are unrelated. If a page describes Robux, survival crafting, or a mobile merge board, it is describing something else.

## How You Actually Play: Hands, Not Controllers

The official FAQ and portfolio page are clear that Dragon Grove uses native hand tracking on Meta Quest 3 and above. There is no controller scheme to learn. You pet dragons, wave to them, cradle them, and match leaves and flowers with your hands directly in your physical space. That is the entire interaction model as advertised.

Because this is mixed reality rather than a fully virtual world, your room becomes part of the experience. That has practical implications the developer does not spell out in detail. Hand tracking accuracy depends on lighting and how clearly your hands are visible to the headset's cameras, so a well-lit room with some clear space in front of you is a reasonable starting assumption. This is general reasoning, not a documented requirement, and you can verify what works in your own setup once the game is in your hands.

## Dragons, Levels, and What Is Documented

The developer has confirmed a specific set of numbers and features. Here is what is verified, kept separate from anything that is merely rumored or inferred.

| Item | What the developer documents |
| --- | --- |
| Starting dragon | Pip, the signature green dragon, for everyone |
| Total dragons at launch | 18, all unlockable through progression |
| Levels at launch | 200+ across 30 unique groves |
| Currency | Coins, earned by completing levels, also purchasable, and they do not expire |
| Shop items | Dragon hats, tail decorations, and auras |
| Optional extras | Dragon toys, which maximize interaction and do not affect gameplay |

Dragon toys are worth a note because they sound gameplay-relevant but are not. They are optional unlockables that let you play fetch or give your dragon a custom perch. They do not change how levels play out. Dragon boosts, by contrast, are power-ups that grant an extra match or an extra ability use. The developer states the game is not pay to win and that every level can be completed without power-ups.

What is not documented is equally important. Beyond Pip, the developer has not published the names of the other 17 dragons at launch. Grove themes, individual plant types, and specific dragon abilities are described only in general terms. There is no published list of level names, coin prices, or star thresholds. If you see a guide claiming those specifics, treat it as unverified until it appears on the official page or in patch notes.

## Advertised Features and the Roadmap

The official page advertises a handful of systems that shape long-term play. Daily streak rewards give a reason to return. Magical charms add another layer to the puzzle space. You can track friends' journeys, which suggests some light social comparison. The puzzles themselves introduce new plant types, layers, and spatial mechanics as you progress, and you can replay groves to earn more stars.

More content is promised through an official roadmap and patch notes, but the developer has not committed to specifics beyond what is listed. Any article that names future dragons, future groves, or a content schedule is going beyond the source material.

Two things are explicitly ruled out. The official FAQ states the art, character assets, and gameplay contain no AI components. And there are no codes, no hotkeys, and no controller bindings, because the game is hand-tracked and there is nothing to bind.

## Practical Reasoning About the Puzzle Loop

No third-party guide has measured data yet, so treat everything below as general reasoning from the matching-puzzle premise rather than tested strategy. In a spatial matching game where you physically place leaves and flowers, the constraint is usually the board state, not your speed. Working from the edges inward tends to keep open space available for later matches. When new layers and spatial mechanics appear, the safest habit is to clear what you can before committing a boost, since boosts that grant an extra match or ability use are most valuable when a board is nearly stuck. Because every level is completable without power-ups, saving Coins for cosmetic items like hats, tail decorations, and auras is a reasonable default if you care about customization more than convenience.

None of this guarantees success. It is a way of thinking about the genre, and the game's own tutorials will teach the real rules.
