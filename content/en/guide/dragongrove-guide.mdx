Search for "Dragon Grove" and you will run into several unrelated things: a Steam survival game called Dragon's Grove, a match-three mobile title, and any number of Roblox experiences with similar names. None of those is the game this article is about. The Dragon Grove covered here is a mixed-reality puzzle game from Schell Games, built in collaboration with Meta and designed specifically for Meta Quest 3 headsets and newer. It is free to play, expected in spring 2027, and it uses native hand tracking instead of controllers. ## What Dragon Grove Actually Is

Per Schell Games' official materials, Dragon Grove is a casual puzzle and entertainment experience built in Unity for mixed reality, aimed at both family and adult players. It is a client project with Meta, which explains the Quest-first platform commitment.

The core loop, as advertised: you match and awaken magical dragon plants, tend a growing grove, unlock dragon abilities, and bond with individual dragons. Matching happens with your hands. You pinch, grab, and place leaves and flowers in physical space, and you pet, wave at, and cradle dragons with the same hand-tracked input. No controller is required, and the FAQ confirms the game is played entirely with native hand tracking.

The tone is deliberately low-key. Schell Games describes it as seated, cozy, and low-stress, which matters if you are deciding whether this fits your setup. This is not an action game that punishes slow hands.

## Is It Pay to Win? The Developer's Answer

Schell Games addresses this directly. Coins can be bought, and the shop sells cosmetics and dragon toys. Dragon boosts are power-ups that give you an extra match or ability use. But the FAQ states that all levels can be completed without power-ups.

That is a meaningful distinction. Buying coins appears to accelerate cosmetics and optional convenience, not gate progress. Whether power-ups make certain late groves noticeably easier is a fair question, but no measured difficulty data exists publicly, so treat any claim about specific hard levels as speculation until the game is out.

The coins-do-not-expire detail is also player-friendly. If you buy currency and drift away for a while, you do not lose it.

## Practical Reasoning for New Players

None of the following is measured data. It is general reasoning from how matching puzzles with limited moves and optional power-ups typically work, applied to what Schell Games has described.

First, expect star ratings to reward efficiency. Replaying groves to earn more stars is an advertised feature, so the game likely scores you on moves used or objectives cleared rather than simply finished-or-not. That suggests you should plan a few matches ahead rather than clearing whatever is closest to your hand.

Second, spatial mechanics and layers imply that match order matters. In 3D matching puzzles, clearing a plant that sits in front of or above others can open new match paths. If a board stalls, look for matches that physically uncover blocked areas instead of taking the most obvious pair.

Third, save dragon boosts. Since every level is completable without them, spending a boost on an early grove is usually a worse trade than holding it for a board where you are genuinely stuck. The same logic applies to Coins: cosmetics are permanent, so there is no rush.

Finally, do not skip the dragon interactions. Dragon toys have no gameplay impact, and the developer is explicit about that, so treat them as flavor rather than optimization. But hand-tracking comfort is a real skill. Spending time petting and cradling dragons early is a low-stakes way to calibrate how your headset reads your hands.
