## What Dragon Grove Is, According to the Developer

Schell Games lists Dragon Grove with a project type of client project with Meta, genre tags of Casual, Puzzle and Entertainment, and a platform of Mixed Reality. It runs on Unity. The audience is family and adult, and the intended experience is seated, cozy and low-stress.

Two design choices define it more than anything else:

- It is built **exclusively for mixed reality**, not virtual reality. That means the game layers its grove onto your real room rather than replacing it.
- It is played **entirely with native hand tracking**. No controllers. You pet dragons, wave to them, cradle them, and match leaves and flowers using your actual hands.

It is free to play, expected in spring 2027, and requires a Meta Quest 3 or newer. That last point matters: older Quest hardware is not listed as supported.

To be clear about what this is not — it is not a Roblox game, not the Steam title Dragon's Grove from Frozen Flame, and not Merge Dragons. Those are separate products that happen to share similar words.

## How the Core Loop Works

The premise, as advertised, is matching leaves and flowers with your hands to awaken magical dragon plants, which in turn helps your grove grow. Completing levels earns Coins. Coins go into the shop for cosmetic items: dragon hats, tail decorations and auras. Coins can also be purchased, and purchased Coins do not expire.

Two systems sit around that loop:

- **Dragon toys** are optional unlockables that let you interact more deeply with your dragon — playing fetch, or giving your dragon a custom perch. The developer explicitly states these have no impact on gameplay.
- **Dragon boosts** are power-ups that grant an extra match or an extra ability use. The developer also explicitly states that all levels can be completed without power-ups.

The studio's framing is that the game is not pay to win. That is a reasonable reading of the official statements, but note that this is a claim from the developer, not something independently verified.

### General Puzzle Reasoning, Not Measured Data

Because no guide data exists yet and hands-on coverage is limited to pre-release impressions, any strategy advice here is reasoning from the matching-puzzle premise rather than tested results.

In most spatial matching games, the reliable habits are:

1. **Scan the whole play space before matching.** In mixed reality you are working in three dimensions, so plants may sit in layers rather than on a flat plane. Finding the full layout first reduces wasted moves.
2. **Prioritize matches that open new areas.** If a match reveals or awakens something new, it usually does more for progress than a match that only clears points.
3. **Hold boosts in reserve.** Since levels are completable without power-ups, sitting on a boost until a level genuinely stalls you is the lower-risk play.
4. **Replay groves deliberately.** Star-based replay is advertised as a way to earn more, so returning to earlier groves is a legitimate path rather than a step backward.

Third-party guides may eventually publish level-by-level walkthroughs. When they do, treat them as secondary observations, not developer documentation. Community write-ups frequently describe strategies that work for the writer's play style and hardware setup, which will not always transfer.

## Hand Tracking Is the Whole Interface

This is the feature most likely to decide whether Dragon Grove clicks for you. Everything — petting, waving, cradling dragons, matching plants — happens through native hand tracking with no controller fallback advertised.

Practically, that means your play space matters. Mixed reality games that rely on hands do better with decent, even lighting, hands clearly within the headset's tracking volume, and enough physical room to move your arms without hitting furniture. The seats-and-cozy framing suggests the game is designed for a relaxed posture rather than standing and swinging, but you should still clear your immediate surroundings before playing.

If hand tracking feels unreliable for you, that is a hardware and environment question first, not a game design one. The Quest 3 and up requirement exists partly because that generation's tracking is what the game was built around.

## Coins, Cosmetics and What You Actually Pay For

The economy as advertised is narrow. Levels give Coins. Coins buy hats, tail decorations and auras. Coins can also be bought with real money and do not expire. Dragon toys unlock through play and add interaction, not power. Boosts add an extra match or ability use and are never required.

What is not documented: coin prices, bundle structures, cosmetic item names, or whether any cosmetic is time-limited. Anyone claiming otherwise without a developer source is guessing. You can verify the actual shop by opening the game and reading the store screen, or by waiting for official patch notes.
