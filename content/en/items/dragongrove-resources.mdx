If you have searched for "DragonGrove" and landed on pages about a Roblox experience, a Steam survival game, or a mobile match-three title, you have been looking at the wrong thing. DragonGrove is a mixed-reality puzzle game from Schell Games, developed as a client project with Meta and built for Meta Quest 3 headsets and up. It is played entirely with native hand tracking — no controller — and it is designed to be seated, cozy, and low-stress. Here is what is actually documented about the game, what third-party guides have observed, and what nobody outside the studio can honestly tell you yet.

## What DragonGrove Actually Is

According to the official Schell Games portfolio page for DragonGrove, the game is a 3D spatial puzzle experience in which you match and awaken magical dragon plants, grow a grove, unlock helpful dragon abilities, and bond with dragons. It is listed as a Casual, Puzzle, and Entertainment title built in Unity, aimed at both family and adult audiences, and it is free to play with an expected launch window of spring 2027.

The platform detail matters more than it sounds. DragonGrove is a Mixed Reality game, which means the puzzle board and your dragons occupy your real room rather than a fully virtual world. You interact with your bare hands: petting dragons, waving to them, cradling them, and matching leaves and flowers by physically reaching out and pairing them. That hand-tracking-first design is the core of the game's identity, and it is why a controller-based comparison to flat-screen matching games misses the point.

Replaying groves is framed as a way to earn more stars, which suggests a star rating per level, though the official material does not publish star thresholds.

## How the Puzzle Loop Likely Works

Schell Games has not published a mechanic-by-mechanic breakdown, so treat the following as general reasoning from the matching-puzzle premise rather than measured data. In a spatial matching game, the difficulty usually comes from three levers: how many distinct plant types are on the board, how many layers or depths those pieces occupy, and how much freedom you have to pair them. Adding a new plant type increases the number of near-misses you have to scan past. Adding a layer means some pieces may be physically behind or above others, which in mixed reality means you may need to lean, reach, or reposition yourself — a genuine spatial constraint rather than a screen-based one.

A reasonable approach for a new player is to clear matches that unlock the most board space first, save dragon boosts for levels where you can see you are one or two matches short, and replay a grove you already enjoy rather than grinding one you dislike. That last point is not a strategy tip so much as a genre observation: cozy puzzle games are usually designed so that repetition is pleasant, and DragonGrove explicitly advertises replaying groves for more stars.

What is not documented: specific dragon abilities, named grove themes beyond what the portfolio page says, individual level names, coin prices, star thresholds, and any patch notes. If you see those details claimed online before launch, they are not from official documentation. You can verify anything you read by checking the game itself once it is available, or by consulting the official DragonGrove page at https://schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove.

## Dragons, Coins, and What Money Actually Buys

DragonGrove has a clear separation between gameplay and cosmetics, at least as advertised. Coins come from completing levels. You spend them in the shop on hats, tail decorations, and auras — all appearance items. You can also buy Coins directly, and they do not expire. Dragon toys are described as optional unlockables that maximize dragon interaction, with no impact on gameplay. Dragon boosts are the only purchase-adjacent item with mechanical effect, and the official FAQ confirms the game can be completed without them.

That structure is worth noting if you are deciding whether to spend anything. Cosmetics and toys support the bonding fantasy the game is selling. Boosts shorten a session. Neither is required to finish the content, so the honest answer to "do I need to pay?" is no, based on the developers' own statement.

One thing the sources do not explain is how many Coins a typical level awards, or what any shop item costs. Those numbers simply are not published, and anyone quoting them is guessing.

## Things People Get Wrong

The most common error is conflating DragonGrove with similarly named games. It is not a Roblox game, it is not the Steam title Dragon's Grove from Frozen Flame, and it is not Merge Dragons. Those are separate products with separate developers and separate business models. If a page describes a Roblox hub, a survival crafting loop, or a mobile merge grid, you are on the wrong page regardless of how the name is spelled.

The second common error is assuming DragonGrove runs on older Quest hardware. The official material specifies Meta Quest 3 and up, which tracks with the hand-tracking and mixed-reality requirements. If you are on Quest 2, the launch platform list does not include you as of the published information.

A third error is treating third-party guides as developer documentation. Community guides can be useful for level walkthroughs once the game is live, but they are secondary observations. When a third-party guide and the official FAQ disagree, trust the FAQ.

## FAQ

The expected window is spring 2027, per the official portfolio page. No specific date has been published.

**How much does it cost?**
The game is free to play. Coins and certain items can be purchased, and Coins do not expire. Specific prices are not documented.

No. DragonGrove is played entirely with native hand tracking.

18 at launch, all unlockable through progression. Everyone begins with Pip, the green dragon.
