If you have seen the phrase "Dragon Grove" floating around and felt confused about which game people mean, you are not alone. Several unrelated titles use similar names, from mobile match-three games to survival crafting projects. This article is about one specific thing: the mixed-reality game Dragon Grove from Schell Games, developed as a client project with Meta. According to the official [Dragon Grove portfolio page](https://schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove), it is a cozy 3D spatial puzzle game built exclusively for Mixed Reality on Meta Quest 3 headsets and up, played entirely with native hand tracking. No controllers, no flat-screen mode. If you were looking for a Roblox experience, the Steam survival game Dragon's Grove, or Merge Dragons, you are in the wrong place, and that confusion is worth clearing up before anything else.

## What Dragon Grove Actually Is

Schell Games describes Dragon Grove as a casual puzzle and entertainment experience in the Mixed Reality category, built in Unity, aimed at both family and adult audiences. It is free to play and expected in spring 2027, with no announced date beyond that season. Player posture is seated, and the studio repeatedly frames the experience as cozy and low-stress.

The core loop, as advertised, works like this: you match and awaken magical dragon plants with your hands, foster a growing grove, unlock helpful dragon abilities, and bond with individual dragons. "Bond" is not marketing fluff here — petting, waving at, and cradling dragons are all performed with tracked hands, which is the entire point of building for Mixed Reality rather than VR controllers.

One identity note that matters because of search results: the official FAQ states the art, character assets, and gameplay contain no AI components. Human artists and designers made what you see.

## Gameplay Reasoning for a Spatial Matching Puzzle

Schell Games has not published a strategy guide, so anything below is general reasoning from the matching-puzzle premise, not measured data. Treat it as a way to think, not a guarantee.

Three-dimensional matching changes the usual calculus. In a flat grid you scan rows and columns; in a spatial grove you also scan depth, which means plants at different layers interact in ways a 2D board cannot replicate. Practical habits that tend to help in this genre: scan the full play space before making an obvious match, save power-ups for moments where you can see the board is nearly solved, and replay earlier groves for stars rather than grinding stuck levels. Because hand tracking replaces a controller, deliberate, slower gestures usually beat fast swipes — the tracking is the interface, and precision matters more than speed. Coins come from completing levels, so cosmetics like hats and tail decorations are a reward for playing, not a shortcut past it.

One thing worth saying plainly: specific dragon abilities and grove themes beyond the official descriptions are not documented anywhere public. If you see a list of named abilities or grove biomes on a third-party site, it is either speculation or datamining, and you should verify it in-game once the title launches.

## Coins, Power-Ups, and the Pay-to-Win Question

This is the question that generates the most noise around free-to-play puzzle games, and Schell Games addressed it directly. Coins are earned by completing levels and can also be purchased; they do not expire. They buy cosmetic items — dragon hats, tail decorations, and auras. Dragon boosts grant an extra match or ability use. Critically, the official FAQ states the game is not pay to win and that all levels can be completed without power-ups.

For a cozy, seated experience aimed partly at families, that framing makes sense. The monetization is cosmetic and convenience-oriented, and the developer has committed in writing that progression does not depend on spending. Whether that holds at launch is something players will be able to check themselves, and it is a claim worth revisiting once the game is actually in headsets.
