Dragon Grove is one of those projects that is easy to misidentify. Search the name and you will find a Roblox experience, a Steam survival game called Dragon's Grove from Frozen Flame, and the mobile puzzler Merge Dragons. None of them are this game. The Dragon Grove covered here is a mixed-reality title from Schell Games, built as a client project with Meta and designed from the ground up for passthrough play on Meta Quest 3 headsets and up. It runs on Unity, targets a family and adult audience, is free to play, and is expected in spring 2027. If you have been trying to work out what this game actually is, what you do in it, and whether it is worth following, the official portfolio page at https://schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove is the reliable starting point.

## What Dragon Grove Actually Is

According to the official Schell Games description, Dragon Grove is a cozy 3D spatial puzzle game built exclusively for Mixed Reality. That phrasing matters. It is not a VR game that happens to show your room, and it is not a flat-screen game with a novelty mode. The mixed-reality framing is the design premise: your real space becomes the setting for a grove of magical plants and dragons.

The genre labels on the official page are Casual, Puzzle, and Entertainment. The session design is seated, cozy, and low-stress. That combination tells you more about the intended experience than any genre tag alone, and it separates Dragon Grove from action-heavy VR titles that assume you are standing, moving, and reacting under pressure.

## Hand Tracking Is the Whole Interface

There is no controller input. Dragon Grove is played entirely with native hand tracking on Meta Quest 3 and above. The official description lists the core interactions directly: you pet dragons, wave to them, cradle them, and match leaves and flowers using your hands.

That is a meaningful design constraint rather than a gimmick. A matching puzzle played with tracked hands in your own room has a different feel from the same puzzle played with a pointer and a trigger. Reach, placement, and physical gesture become part of the loop. If you want to know how precise that tracking needs to be before you commit, the honest answer is that the official sources describe the interactions but do not publish tracking tolerances, latency figures, or calibration guidance. Those details are not documented, and the only reliable way to judge them is to play the game when it arrives.

## The Puzzle Layer, Reasoned From the Premise

The matching premise is documented: you match leaves and flowers, and the game advertises 3D matching puzzles with new plant types, layers, and spatial mechanics. What is not documented is how any specific level is laid out, how many moves you get, or how stars are scored. Third-party guides may eventually fill some of that in, but as of now there is no developer documentation for individual levels, and anything claiming otherwise is speculation.

What you can reasonably infer from the structure is general matching-puzzle logic rather than measured data. Three-dimensional layouts with layers usually mean some pieces are partially occluded and become available only after pieces in front of them are cleared. A spatial matching game played with your hands also tends to reward deliberate placement over speed, which fits the cozy, low-stress framing. None of that is a guaranteed strategy, and Schell Games has not published a scoring rubric.

## Progression, Coins, and the Pay-to-Win Question

Progression reportedly runs on Coins earned by completing levels, spent in a shop on cosmetic items such as dragon hats, tail decorations, and auras. Coins can also be purchased, and the official FAQ states they do not expire. Dragon toys are framed as optional unlockables that maximise dragon interaction, letting you play fetch or give a dragon a custom perch, with no impact on gameplay.

The important claim is the one about difficulty. According to the official FAQ, all levels can be completed without power-ups. That is a direct statement from the developer that the game is not pay to win. It is not an independent audit, and it is not a promise about how hard any individual level will feel to you, but it is the developer's own position and the clearest guidance available.

## FAQ

**Is Dragon Grove a Roblox game?**
No. It is a mixed-reality game from Schell Games made in collaboration with Meta, for Meta Quest 3 headsets and up.

**Do I need controllers?**
No. It is played entirely with native hand tracking.

**When does it come out?**
The official expectation is spring 2027. No specific day has been announced.

**Is it pay to win?**
The official FAQ states all levels can be completed without power-ups. Coins buy cosmetics and can be purchased, and they do not expire.

**How many dragons are there?**
Eighteen at launch, all unlockable through progression, starting with Pip.

**Does it use AI-generated art?**
The official FAQ states the art, character assets, and gameplay contain no AI components.
