If you have searched for "Dragon Grove" lately, you have probably landed on a Roblox experience, a Steam survival game, or a match-three mobile title. None of those are the Dragon Grove this article covers. The Dragon Grove discussed here is a mixed-reality game from Schell Games, built as a client project with Meta and designed specifically for spatial puzzle play on Meta Quest headsets. You can see the official project page at [Schell Games' Dragon Grove portfolio entry](https://schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove).

Here is what is actually documented about the game, what remains unannounced, and how to reason about it as a player before launch.

## What Dragon Grove Is, According to the Developer

Schell Games describes Dragon Grove as a cozy 3D spatial puzzle game made exclusively for mixed reality. It is a casual entertainment title built in Unity, aimed at a family and adult audience, and played on Meta Quest 3 headsets and up. It uses native hand tracking, meaning you play without a controller: you pet, wave to, and cradle dragons, and you match leaves and flowers using your hands in physical space.

It is free to play, with an expected release in spring 2027. The experience is seated, low-stress, and cozy by design. That combination matters. Many mixed-reality games ask you to stand, move around a room, or clear a play boundary. Dragon Grove instead frames itself as something you can settle into, which fits the hand-tracking-only control scheme and the relaxed tone the developer advertises.

If you want the developer's own framing, the official FAQ answers common questions directly.

## The Core Loop: Matching, Awakening, Bonding

The gameplay premise, as advertised, is that you match and awaken magical dragon plants. Doing so helps you foster your grove, unlock helpful dragon abilities, and bond with dragons. That is the fundamental loop: solve spatial matching puzzles, grow your space, and build relationships with the creatures that inhabit it.

The official page also advertises daily streak rewards, magical charms, meeting new dragons, tracking friends' journeys, 3D matching puzzles with new plant types, layers, and spatial mechanics, and replaying groves to earn more stars.

It is worth being precise about what "abilities" and "grove themes" mean here. The developer confirms that dragons grant helpful abilities and that groves exist as distinct spaces, but the specific abilities and the specific themes are not documented on the official pages. Anyone claiming to list every dragon ability or name every grove at this stage is working beyond what has been published. The honest answer is that those details are unannounced.

## Coins, Hats, and What "Not Pay to Win" Means

Completing levels earns Coins. Coins are spent in the shop on cosmetic items the developer describes as dragon hats, tail decorations, and auras. Coins can also be purchased, and purchased Coins do not expire.

Two categories of optional items deserve separation, because they are easy to confuse:

- **Dragon toys** are optional unlockables that maximize dragon interaction. The examples given are playing fetch and giving your dragon a custom perch. The developer states plainly that toys have no impact on gameplay.
- **Dragon boosts** are power-ups that grant an extra match or an extra ability use.

Schell Games explicitly says the game is not pay to win, and that all levels can be completed without power-ups. That is a meaningful design commitment and worth taking at face value, but it is also the kind of claim only full play can test. If you are the sort of player who wants to confirm it yourself, the practical approach is simple: play a stretch of levels using no boosts, and see whether progress ever stalls in a way that only a purchase could fix. That is verifiable in the game rather than taken on faith.

## Reasoning About the Puzzles Before You Play

Schell Games has not published level names, plant names, star thresholds, or coin prices, and this article will not invent them. But you can reason sensibly about a 3D spatial matching puzzle from its premise alone, and that reasoning is useful preparation rather than measured strategy.

Spatial matching in mixed reality usually means the arrangement of objects in three dimensions matters, not just the color or type of the object. The developer's own mention of "new plant types, layers, and spatial mechanics" suggests that later groves introduce depth and layering as variables rather than simply adding more of the same pieces. A reasonable expectation, then, is that early groves teach you what counts as a match, and later groves ask you to think about where things sit relative to each other.

General habits that tend to help in matching puzzles of this shape:

- Scan the whole space before your first move, including pieces tucked behind or beneath others.
- Watch for plant types that appear rarely early on, since they often become the constraint later.
- Replay groves you have already cleared. The developer advertises replaying groves to earn more stars, so earlier content retains value.
- Keep daily streaks going if rewards matter to you, since streak rewards are an advertised feature.

None of this guarantees anything. It is reasoning from the premise, not data from the build.
