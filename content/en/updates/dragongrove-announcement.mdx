If you have searched for "Dragon Grove" and landed on pages about a Roblox experience, a Steam survival game called Dragon's Grove, or the mobile match-3 Merge Dragons, you are in the wrong grove. Dragon Grove is a mixed-reality puzzle game from Schell Games, built as a client project with Meta. You play it seated, with your bare hands, on a Meta Quest 3 headset or newer. This article separates what the developer has actually confirmed from what third-party guides claim and what nobody knows yet.

The official product page at [schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove](https://schellgames.com/portfolio/dragon-grove) is the authority here. Everything below is drawn from that page and Schell Games' published FAQ unless explicitly labeled otherwise.

## What Dragon Grove Actually Is

Dragon Grove is a cozy 3D spatial matching puzzle game designed exclusively for Mixed Reality. That word "exclusively" matters. This is not a VR game with an MR mode bolted on, and it is not a flatscreen game. The design assumes your real room is part of the play space.

It runs on Unity, targets the Mixed Reality platform, and is aimed at a family and adult audience. It will be free to play with an expected launch in spring 2027. There is no announced specific date beyond that window, and anyone quoting a precise day is guessing.

The genre labels Schell Games uses are casual, puzzle, and entertainment. The studio also describes it as seated, cozy, and low-stress. If you are looking for a twitch-reflex game, this is not it.

A crucial detail for buyers: Dragon Grove requires a Meta Quest 3 or above. Older Quest headsets are not listed as supported. Play is entirely through native hand tracking, with no controller required. You pet dragons, wave at them, cradle them, and match leaves and flowers using your actual hands.

## Hand Tracking, MR Play, and Comfort

Because the game runs on native hand tracking, the practical setup questions are different from a controller-based game. You will want reasonable lighting so the headset cameras can track your hands, and clear space around your play area so you do not swipe into furniture while matching.

Schell Games designed the experience for seated play. That is a meaningful accessibility choice: it lowers fatigue, reduces room-scale requirements, and fits the cozy framing.

Note that Schell Games has not published a detailed list of tracked gestures beyond petting, waving, cradling, and matching. If you want to know how a specific interaction feels, the honest answer is that you need to try it or watch developer footage rather than trust a list of invented gestures.

## Puzzle Strategy: General Reasoning, Not Measured Data

Schell Games has not published level solutions, star thresholds, or scoring formulas. Anything you read claiming exact star cutoffs or optimal move counts is unverified. What follows is general reasoning from the matching-puzzle premise, not tested data.

Start by scanning the whole arrangement before you touch anything. Matching puzzles reward pattern recognition over speed, and the developer's own framing is low-stress, so there is no reason to rush a move.

Identify what the puzzle is actually asking. Because advertised mechanics include layers and spatial dimensions, a match may not be about two adjacent items on one flat plane. Look for alignment opportunities that only exist when you consider depth.

Work from the constrained areas outward. In most matching designs, the positions with the fewest possible partners are the ones that determine whether you can clear a board. Freeing those early tends to open options elsewhere.

Play new plant types deliberately the first time you see them. The official page mentions new plant types arriving as you progress, which implies the rules layer on over time rather than all at once. Treat each new type as a small tutorial moment.

Do not feel obligated to spend power-ups to progress. The developer states plainly that all levels are completable without them, so hoarding boosts for a genuinely stuck board is a reasonable approach.
